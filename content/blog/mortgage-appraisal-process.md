# Understanding the Mortgage Appraisal Process for Homebuyers

If you are financing a home purchase, the appraisal is one of the key steps between an accepted offer and closing. Your lender uses it to evaluate the property that will secure the loan, and the result can affect how much you can borrow and how the rest of the transaction unfolds.

This guide explains how the mortgage appraisal process works, what the appraiser does, how the appraised value affects your loan, and what your options are if the value comes in lower than expected.

## Why Lenders Require an Appraisal

A mortgage is secured by the home you are buying. Before lending, the lender needs an independent opinion of the property's market value to understand the collateral behind the loan. The appraisal also documents the property's characteristics and condition, which helps the lender confirm that the home meets the requirements of the loan program.

Some loans qualify for an appraisal waiver or another valuation alternative, depending on the loan program and the lender's process. Your loan officer can tell you whether a full appraisal is required for your loan.

## Who Orders the Appraisal — and Who Chooses the Appraiser

For a mortgage, **the lender orders the appraisal**, often through an appraisal management company. Appraiser independence rules are designed to keep the valuation impartial, so borrowers, real estate agents, and loan officers generally cannot select the appraiser or pressure the appraiser toward a particular value.

The borrower usually pays the appraisal fee, often at the start of the loan process. Fees vary with the property, the type of report, and the loan program; your lender will disclose the cost in your loan estimate.

## Step by Step: How the Mortgage Appraisal Works

![Appraiser inspecting a residential property](/images/blog/mortgage-appraisal-process/appraiser-inspecting-home.jpg)

1. **The lender orders the appraisal** after your loan application is underway and the purchase contract is in place.
2. **The appraiser schedules the inspection** with the person who controls access to the home — typically the seller or the listing agent for a purchase.
3. **The appraiser inspects the property**, observing the interior and exterior, noting features, updates, and condition, measuring the home, and taking photographs.
4. **The appraiser researches the market**, including recent sales of comparable homes, active and pending listings, and market trends.
5. **The appraiser analyzes the data and writes the report**, applying the valuation approaches that fit the property and reconciling them into an opinion of market value.
6. **The lender reviews the report** as part of underwriting.

Timing varies with the property, the market, and the lender's process. If your contract has an appraisal-related deadline, make sure your lender and agent know it at the start.

## How the Appraised Value Affects Your Loan

Lenders typically base the maximum loan amount on the property's value. For a purchase, that is generally the **lower of the purchase price or the appraised value**, combined with the maximum loan-to-value ratio allowed by your loan program.

Here is a simplified example. Suppose you agree to buy a home for $400,000 and plan to borrow 90 percent:

| Scenario | Value used by lender | 90% loan | Cash needed beyond the loan |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Appraisal at $400,000 | $400,000 | $360,000 | $40,000 |
| Appraisal at $390,000 | $390,000 | $351,000 | $49,000 |

In the second scenario, the price did not change, but the loan amount did — so the buyer would need to bring more cash, renegotiate, or consider other options. (This example omits closing costs and assumes the program allows 90 percent financing.)

### Condition and "subject to" appraisals

If the appraiser observes issues that affect safety, soundness, or the loan program's property requirements, the appraisal may be completed **"subject to"** specific repairs or completion of work. In that case, the lender may require the work to be finished and inspected before closing, sometimes with a brief follow-up inspection by the appraiser.

## What If the Appraisal Comes in Low?

![Homebuyer reviewing an appraisal report](/images/blog/mortgage-appraisal-process/reviewing-appraisal-report.jpg)

A value below the contract price is sometimes called an "appraisal gap." Common options include:

- **Renegotiating** the price with the seller
- **Covering the difference** in cash
- **Restructuring** the financing, if your loan program allows
- **Terminating the contract**, if your contract and any addenda give you that right

In Texas, whether you can terminate — and by when — depends on the terms of your purchase contract and any financing or appraisal addenda. Your real estate agent or attorney can explain your specific rights.

### Requesting a reconsideration of value

If you believe the report contains factual errors — for example, the wrong square footage or bedroom count — or overlooked relevant comparable sales, ask your lender about its **reconsideration of value (ROV)** process. Requests go through the lender, not directly to the appraiser, and the most effective ones are specific and supported by data available as of the appraisal's effective date.

## You Are Entitled to a Copy

Federal rules generally require your lender to give you a copy of the appraisal promptly, and no later than three business days before closing unless you have waived that timing. Read it carefully: our guide on [how to read an appraisal report](/blog/how-to-read-an-appraisal-report/) walks through each section.

## How the Seller Can Help the Inspection Go Smoothly

For a purchase, the seller usually controls the property during the appraisal. Practical steps include providing access to every room, the attic, the garage, and mechanical equipment; keeping pets secured; and preparing a list of significant improvements with approximate dates. Cleaning and decluttering make the inspection easier but do not by themselves change market value. Learn more in [What Is a Home Appraisal and Why Does It Matter?](/blog/what-is-a-home-appraisal/)

## Appraisals Outside the Mortgage Process

Not every appraisal is ordered by a lender. Homeowners, sellers, attorneys, and executors can engage an appraiser directly for purposes such as a [pre-purchase appraisal](/pre-purchase-appraisal/), a [pre-listing appraisal](/pre-listing-appraisal/), or [PMI removal](/pmi-removal-appraisal/).
