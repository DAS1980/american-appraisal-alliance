# How to Read Your Appraisal Report: A Clear Guide

An appraisal report does more than state a number. A credible report explains what was appraised, which market data the appraiser relied on, how that data was analyzed, and why the final opinion of value is supported. Knowing where to look makes the report far easier to understand — whether you are a homeowner, buyer, seller, attorney, or real estate professional.

This guide walks through the major sections found in most residential appraisal reports. If you are new to appraisals, you may want to start with [what a home appraisal is and why it matters](/blog/what-is-a-home-appraisal/).

## First, Note the Report Type

Most mortgage appraisals for single-family homes are prepared on the **Uniform Residential Appraisal Report (URAR)**, a standardized form used for loans sold to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Those agencies are transitioning to a redesigned URAR under an updated Uniform Appraisal Dataset (UAD 3.6), so the layout of your report may differ from older examples you find online. Appraisals for private purposes — such as divorce, estate, or pre-listing assignments — may use a different format.

Whatever the format, every appraisal report prepared under the **Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice (USPAP)** must identify the client, the intended use, the type and definition of value, the effective date, and the scope of work. Read these first: they tell you what question the appraisal answers.

## The Subject Property Section

![Appraisal report and market data under review](/images/blog/how-to-read-an-appraisal-report/market-data-review.jpg)

This section describes the property being appraised — the "subject." Look for:

- **Identification:** address, legal description, and parcel number
- **Site:** lot size, zoning, utilities, and any easements or adverse conditions noted
- **Improvements:** year built, gross living area, room count, bedrooms, bathrooms, garage, and notable features
- **Condition and quality:** the appraiser's observations about maintenance, updates, and construction quality

Check the facts carefully. If the square footage, bedroom count, or a major feature is wrong, that is the kind of factual error worth raising with the party who ordered the appraisal.

### Condition and quality ratings

Many lender reports express condition and quality using standardized ratings — for example, C1 through C6 for condition, where lower numbers indicate better condition. The report should describe the observations that support the rating, such as the age of kitchen and bathroom updates or deferred maintenance.

## The Neighborhood and Market Conditions

This part of the report describes the area around the subject property and the direction of the market: whether values are increasing, stable, or declining; how supply compares with demand; and typical marketing times. These conditions matter because an appraisal reflects the market as of a specific **effective date**.

## The Sales Comparison Approach and Adjustment Grid

For most homes, this is the heart of the report. The appraiser selects recent sales of similar homes — the "comparables" or "comps" — and presents them side by side with the subject property in an adjustment grid.

How to read the grid:

1. **Compare the basics.** Look at location, distance from the subject, sale date, size, age, and condition. Good comparables are as similar as the market allows.
2. **Read the adjustments.** When a comparable is superior to the subject in some respect, the appraiser makes a downward adjustment to its sale price; when it is inferior, the adjustment is upward. The goal is to estimate what each comparable would have sold for if it were more like the subject.
3. **Review the adjusted sale prices.** After adjustments, the comparables should point to a reasonably consistent range of value.
4. **Read the commentary.** The appraiser should explain why particular sales were chosen and how significant adjustments were supported.

A reasonable question to ask is not "Is there a higher sale nearby?" but "Is this comparable truly similar, and was it available to the appraiser as of the effective date?"

## The Cost and Income Approaches

![Discussion of property valuation approaches](/images/blog/how-to-read-an-appraisal-report/valuation-approaches-discussion.jpg)

Depending on the property and assignment, the report may also include:

- **The cost approach:** estimated land value plus the cost to construct the improvements new, less depreciation. It is often given more weight for newer or unique homes.
- **The income approach:** a value indication based on rental income, most relevant for rental and multi-unit properties.

If an approach was not developed, the report should explain why.

## Reconciliation and the Final Opinion of Value

In reconciliation, the appraiser weighs the reliability of each approach and the quality of the data, then states a final opinion of value as of the effective date. Pay attention to:

- **The effective date** — the date the value applies to, which can differ from the date the report was signed
- **Conditions or assumptions** — for example, a value "subject to" completion of repairs, or an extraordinary assumption
- **The certification** — the appraiser's signed statements of impartiality and compliance with USPAP

## How Long Is an Appraisal Valid?

An appraisal states value as of its effective date; it does not expire on its own, but markets change. Lenders and loan programs set their own requirements for how recent an appraisal must be, and an older appraisal may require a [market value update or recertification](/market-value-update-1004d-recertification/). For private purposes, a new appraisal is appropriate when market conditions or the property itself have changed.

## What If You Disagree With the Report?

For a mortgage appraisal, communicate through your lender rather than contacting the appraiser directly. Many lenders have a formal reconsideration of value (ROV) process. The most useful requests are specific: factual errors in the property description, or relevant comparable sales that were available as of the effective date and are not discussed in the report.

For an appraisal you engaged privately, contact the appraiser with your questions. Keep in mind that an appraiser's fee cannot be contingent on reaching a particular value, and the appraiser must remain impartial.
